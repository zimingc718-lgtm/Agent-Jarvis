Data Center World 2026: Power Architecture Pushed Beyond the Rack

The move toward 800 VDC and new power architectures stems from mounting constraints in how compute, cooling, and power fit inside the rack.

Shane Snider,Senior News Writer,Data Center Knowledge

April 22, 2026

4 Min Read
Higher‑voltage DC is emerging less as a pure efficiency play and more as a way to reclaim rack space for GPUs, Schneider Electric’s CTO told Data Center World 2026.Photo by Shane Snider

Washington, DC — AI workloads are driving rack power densities to new highs, exposing physical limits inside the rack and forcing operators to rethink how energy is delivered to compute.

That was the message from Jim Simonelli, senior vice president and CTO of Schneider Electric’s Secure Power and Data Center business, during a keynote at Data Center World 2026.

Simonelli argued that the industry’s debate over AC vs. DC misses the bigger constraint: the rack's internal real estate. As operators place more GPUs closer together to serve token-hungry AI models, power and cooling hardware are competing with compute for space.

“If we believe that serving AI demand with more tokens per second … scale-up is valuable, we will be putting more GPUs closer together,” he said, adding that the result is higher power density. “That’s the challenge we’re trying to face.”

AI Density Is Colliding With Rack Design

As rack power climbs, Simonelli said the internal layout is becoming a limiting factor. Power delivery hardware is no longer just supporting compute – it is competing with it. “There’s too much stuff in that rack – copper, power supplies – things that are not driving compute,” he said. “They’re just getting in the way.”

Related:Inside the Push to Bring DC Power to Data Centers

At higher densities, those tradeoffs become harder to manage. Larger conductors are required to carry more current, increasing cable bulk and routing complexity. In parallel, liquid-cooling components – piping, manifolds, and heat exchangers – are expanding inside the same footprint.

The result, Simonelli said, is a stacking problem: more GPUs require more power and more cooling, but the infrastructure to support both begins to crowd out usable compute space.

800 VDC Targets Space and Efficiency

Within that context, Simonelli positioned 800 VDC architectures as a response to rack-level constraints as much as an efficiency upgrade. Raising the voltage reduces the current for a given power level, enabling smaller conductors and less copper in the rack and reducing the number of conversion steps across the power chain.

“The motivation is how to create more space for the GPUs to do what they need to do,” he said, emphasizing efforts to minimize cabling and move power conversion equipment out of the rack.

But Simonelli cautioned against framing the shift as a simple AC-to-DC transition or a single architectural decision. “It’s not just around a single architecture,” he said. “It’s more complex than that.” Instead, he described higher-voltage DC as one element of a broader redesign of how power is delivered, converted, and distributed across increasingly dense compute environments.

Related:Data Center World 2026: As AI Scale Surges, a Call to Build for Legacy

Sidecar Designs Address Near-Term Constraints

One near-term approach is the sidecar (adjacent power-rack) model, which relocates power conversion equipment outside the compute rack and feeds it with smaller, simpler cabling. “Can I pull some of the power conversion outside of the compute rack … and then bring in smaller cables to support that compute?” Simonelli asked.

By keeping most AC infrastructure intact and limiting DC distribution to a localized zone, operators can introduce change incrementally, especially in existing facilities. But he framed sidecars as a stepping stone rather than an end state. “If you’re deploying fleets of racks … taking up valuable white space with power racks is likely not where you want to end up,” he said.

As densities rise, Simonelli expects more power conversion to move farther from the rack, potentially into centralized systems that distribute higher-voltage DC across larger sections of the data center.

A Portfolio of Architectures Is Emerging

Rather than a single dominant design, Simonelli sees a portfolio approach that varies by workload, redundancy, and whether the site is greenfield or a retrofit.

Related:Geothermal and Storage: The Next Frontier in Reliable Data Center Power

Smaller clusters may continue to rely on localized conversion, while larger deployments could justify more centralized, higher-voltage distribution schemes. The result is a fragmented but flexible landscape, where multiple approaches coexist.

Distribution and Safety Remain Open Questions

The tougher questions, Simonelli noted, are on distribution and protection. “You can generate the power – we all know how to create these things,” he said. “But can I distribute it in a safe, effective, and reliable manner?”

Delivering higher-voltage DC at scale changes how operators think about fault isolation, arc-flash risk, and system coordination. These are not new problems, but they take on different characteristics at higher voltages and power densities.

Simonelli emphasized that safety procedures, operational practices, and standards development will be as important as advances in power electronics.

Scaling Will Decide the Outcome

Ultimately, scalability will determine which architectures prevail. “It’s not always the best technology that wins – it’s the one that can scale,” Simonelli said. That includes supply chain readiness, component availability, and the ability to integrate new designs into existing operational models. Even technically viable approaches may struggle if they cannot be manufactured, deployed, and maintained consistently across large fleets.

In that sense, the move toward higher-voltage DC is both technical and operational – requiring coordination across design, manufacturing, and data center operations.
