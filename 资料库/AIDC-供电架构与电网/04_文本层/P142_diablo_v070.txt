4 Compliance with OCP Tenets  
4.1 Openness 
This specification will hopefully serve as the future fundamental base for a 
disaggregated power rack to deliver ±400VDC to a nearby IT rack. The base of the 
mechanical rack follows the Open Rack standard. Currently three companies have 
worked together to provide a high-level overview of the Diablo 400V architecture. The 
goal is to standardize items such as, high voltage connectors and mating interfaces.  
4.2 Efficiency  
Transferring power at a higher voltage is more efficient, reduces I2R losses. This 
disaggregated power rack can be used with multiple generations and SKUs of IT racks; 
a disaggregated power rack can remain in the same location in the datacenter while IT 
===== PAGE 10 =====
Open Compute Project •  Diablo 400 
Date:  March 1st , 2026         Page 10 
racks can be swapped as required. This will ultimately reduce the overall infrastructure 
costs. One objective is to use learnings from this architecture to distribute increased 
direct current voltage in the datacenter directly to the IT racks to improve overall end to 
end power distribution efficiency. 
4.3 Impact 
This is the first disaggregated power rack specification. Standardizing the high voltage 
rack architecture will hopefully lead to a common supply chain, provide end to end power 
efficiency gains, and leverage mainstream EV industry technologies of increased direct 
current voltage as ±400VDC or 800Vdc from charging/battery infrastructure.    
4.4 Scale 
The fundamental advantage of a disaggregated power rack is scalability in terms of 
rated power, redundancy schemes and backup power.  
4.5 Sustainability 
Disaggregated power rack has the ability to be scaled for various power ratings allowing 
it to be re-used for multiple generations of an IT rack. The IT rack can be updated while 
maintaining the same hardware in the disaggregated power rack, therefore reducing the 
need to scrap power building blocks.

5 Scope 
With increased rack power (from 33kW to 100’s of kW), size and power of AI 
accelerators, and rack/POD size growth, there are significant challenges with 
interconnect density, power delivery and overall system integration.  To solve this, the 
disaggregated power rack (also known as sidecar) provides a flexible and evolving 
infrastructure solution.    
The disaggregated power rack can be scalable and updated to meet various power 
requirements from 800kW to 1MW+ (with the option to parallelize power racks together) 
while keeping the disaggregated IT rack the same. 
The rack shall support different power distribution configuration options. A few examples 
are provided in Figure 1, for illustration purposes, not as a final design solution.

7.1.3 AC Loss (Holdup and ride-trough) 
Holdup notion applies to built-in PSU bulk storage that allows time until other form of 
energy support is engaged and activated to support the rack functions in case of AC 
loss. Separate from holdup, the ride-through capability of the PSU defines running 
without interruptions during voltage dropouts and sags/swells. 
7.1.3.1 PSU ride-through without energy storage 
Line Overvoltage: Follow ITIC curve for overvoltage and requires PSU to ride-through 
and not latch-off. 
Line Sag: Follow both ITIC and IEC 61000-4-34 and requires PSU to ride-through and 
not latch-off. 
Minimum holdup without energy storage requirement is up to 20msec at 100% loading. 
Addition of system distributed holdup is acceptable, between the AC/DC PSU in the 
Diablo 400 rack and with the downstream DC/DC converter, located outside the rack. 
 
7.1.3.2 PSU ride-through with energy storage 
Shall ride-through any duration within the energy storage  hold up time range. 
The PSU/Shelf hold-up energy/time shall be enough covering the smooth transition from 
AC drop out to energy storage ramp up at the worst-case condition, which can be the AC 
drop at the lowest point of bulk voltage during peak load as defined in 9.3.2.    
Holdup with energy storage requirement shall be decided per solution specific 
requirements, sufficient to cover transition time. 
 
7.2 Energy Storage Solutions 
The rack is designed to support various storage solutions such as battery backup unit 
(BBU) and capacitor bank energy storage units (CBU). Depending on the user’s 
requirement the number of BBUs and CBUs can be adjusted. 
7.2.1 BBU Option 
Rack shall support BBU options. Design shall support power ranges per electrical 
specification. 
• Optional active management via microcontroller for secure access  
• Optional module interface with a Rack Management Unit -interface standard

the AC/DC PSU module or/and power shelf.

7.4 Multi Rack Parallel Option 
Power rack shall have optional design features that enable paralleling multiple racks to 
increase power delivery. 
Current sharing between racks including droop and active sharing shall be implemented 
at AC/DC power shelf level or/and rack level. 
Synchronization and control features shall be implemented to ensure synchronized 
power up and power down for the case of multi-rack parallel option. 
8 Power Rack Building Blocks 
8.1 AC & DC PDU 
The 3 phase AC input and DC ±400VDC Output Protection and Control Modules are 
estimated to take up to 15OU of space and described below. 
8.1.1 AC PDU Configuration 
The AC PDU takes in the AC power from the overhead busway and outputs power to 
each of 3 phase power shelves inputs.  
The AC PDU shall be designed to: 
• Accept input connection to quantity up to 12x200A cords 
• Include protection fuses 
• Accept either input connection with two-hole compression lugs or connectorized 
• Include an optional AC Panel/Cabinet  
The output of the AC PDU 3-phase power is split into smaller rated cables (ex. 200A to 
4x50A) cables to be routed to various power shelves to power the 3-phase PSUs.

8.1.2  ±400VDC Power Distribution 
The output of the ±400VDC from the power shelves is collected and distributed via a 
vertical busbar spanning the full height of the rack.  The source of the ±400V is 
generated by the power shelf or energy storage devices and is transferred via the rack 
busbar which also is connected to the Output Protection and Control Modules.   
8.1.3  ±400VDC PDU Output Protection and Control Module 
The DC PDU Module shall be designed to support up to 400kW output, with built in 
manageability, output On/Off, protection and telemetry, and no voltage contact on the 
output cables.  
• The Output Protection and Control Module shall attach to the ±400Vdc or 800Vdc 
busbar and contain several output connectors that will transfer the power to the IT 
rack via cables 
• Multiple output cables per output module, based on two options: 
o Option 1: 50kW per cable, qty as needed to support output power range 
o Option 2: 100kW per cable, qty as needed to support output power range 
• The front of the Output Protection and Control Module shall contain physical on/off 
switches to turn on/off the corresponding output connector. 
• The Output Protection and Control Module shall contain LED indicators, fuses and 
relays to protect each output connection in case of over current.  
• Output voltage disconnect-each output shall disconnect the voltage on the connector 
before the cable is unplugged from either source or load side and when no output 
cable is installed. 
• Optional active management via microcontroller, for secure access 
• Optional module interface with a Rack Management Unit-interface standard defined 
in section 9

This section presents a simple list of pros and cons related to two types of PE grounding 
methods. 
12.1 High Resistance Grounding (HRG) 
 No arcing or fire risk when +/-400V short to PE 
 No serious injury to human body if touching +/-400V due to limited leakage current 
🟡Increased spacing requirements of downstream DCDC -to be confirmed by DCDC 
Designer/safety. 
🟡Complex design and maintenance 
Pro1: No electric shock risk if touching +400 V or - 400 V with reference to ground.  
Electric shock risk still exists between +400 to -400. 
Pro2: Mitigates arcs due to a line to ground fault.  Note: does not protect against line-to-
line faults 
Con1: Electric shock risk if touching  +400 V or - 400 V under single fault condition 
where the other leg faults to ground 
Con2: Fault detection is required to effectively resolve a fault to ground before a second 
fault occurs.  
Con3: Putting fault detection at payload rack tray level may be expensive. Having fault 
detection on per rack level (or even Sidecar level) will be difficult to troubleshoot. 
Con4: First fault to ground will not disrupt operation and therefore is at risk of being a 
lower priority to resolve. Second fault to ground can create a fire/electric shock risk. 
Con5: Sensors must be calibrated and tested on a recurring basis.  A defective sensor 
must be replaced immediately.  If the sensors are not operating properly this could give 
you a false sense of security. 
Con6: Multiple sources in parallel would reduce the impedance to ground and increase 
the touch current 
Con7: A component in a payload rack that was designed for high impedance grounding 
scheme may not be suitable for use in a low impedance grounding scheme.  If the 
component is deployed in a Data Center with DC bus but it does not employ a high 
impedance grounding scheme, careful review of the component must be done to ensure 
compatibility.  For example, a component designed for use in a high impedance 
grounding system may not have input fuses or functional insulation may have been used 
between the 400V to chassis.

Let Icc(u) and Vcc(u) be Random Variables, able to take any value from a universe of

Let function E{ } be Expected Value, i.e., average, of a given Random Variable being 
operated on. 
 
A = E{Icc(u) at GF} = Iavg 
A+x = I_Pk = E{Icc(u) Worst Case Max}  
  
 
 
 
Implementations of GPUs CPUs Average (Thermal) Power algorithms could measure 
and average the processor power.  Within the processor, the dominant power is the 
Dynamic Power which is linear with Current, i.e., Pdyn = Idyn * Vcc, where Idyn = C * 
Vcc * Freq., the controlled and measured Processor Average Power will render a 
resultant Average Current proportional to Average Power.
